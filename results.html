Section 01 — Sea Level Rise
Projected Global Sea Level Rise by Scenario

Using the BRICK model, we generated annual regional sea level projections from 2015 to 2100 under each SSP scenario. As expected, higher-emissions pathways produce significantly greater sea level rise, with divergence between scenarios becoming pronounced after mid-century. Note that under any scenario, a certain amount of sea level rise is to be expected, and even "locked-in". Actions that we take now don't undo our prior negligence, so a certain level of rise is inevitable.

Section 02 — Economic Costs
Projected Global Economic Damages by Scenario

Using the pyCIAM model with our BRICK sea level projections and the SLIIDERS coastal dataset, we estimated total global economic damages for each SSP scenario in 2100. Costs span six categories: permanent inundation, storm damage, mortality, protection expenditures, relocation, and wetland loss. We calculate the costs under the "optimal" scenario, which is the least-cost adaptation response, for each SSP. With the exception of SSP3-7.0, which we attribute to compressed sea level projections in our BRICK output for that scenario, optimal adaptation costs scale monotonically with emissions pathway, ranging from $0.84T under SSP1-2.6 to $1.19T under SSP5-8.5.

Protection infrastructure accounts for the largest share of optimal adaptation costs across all scenarios — roughly 46–47% of total damages — suggesting that the dominant economic burden of sea level rise is not direct flooding, but the cost of defending against it.

Across all four scenarios, coastal protection dominates total costs, followed by relocation and inundation. This pattern holds regardless of emissions pathway, suggesting that the structure of adaptation costs is driven more by the geography of coastal assets than by the magnitude of sea level rise itself. SSP5-8.5 produces the highest total damages at $1.19T annually by 2100, with protection costs alone reaching $0.51T.

The anomalously low costs under SSP3-7.0 relative to SSP2-4.5 are likely attributable to compressed sea level projections from our TAS emulator for that scenario rather than a genuine economic finding, and should be interpreted with caution.
